# Chapter 8 — Small Departmental Engagement

James River County Permitting Department is wholly fictional. Every price, hour, stage, rule, and result below is fixture-dependent educational modeling—not a government-sales benchmark.

## 1. Why smaller sounds attractive

Chapter 8 changes `CONTRACT SIZE`, not the customer. One correction-desk team receives a read-only, configuration-first view rather than a broad custom implementation. Fewer users, dependencies, and privileges plausibly reduce delivery and review work. They also reduce revenue.

```text
                   SMALLER CONTRACT

      LOWER DELIVERY              LOWER REVENUE
          COST                        |
```

## 2. The acquisition floor

The floor is not a universal minimum. It is the sum of the fixture's required non-implementation stages: sponsor coordination, bounded discovery, scope validation, technical/access review, purchasing authorization, short proposal/SOW, contract coordination, implementation authorization, and acceptance. The baseline totals **58 acquisition hours**. Shrinking delivery does not silently discount this work.

```text
EASIER TO APPROVE
        ≠
ECONOMIC TO SELL
```

## 3. Define the small departmental scope

`SMALL_DEPARTMENTAL` means eight modeled users on the Permitting Operations correction desk, one correction/resubmission reconciliation slice, an approved read-only export, and configuration-first mappings. Included capabilities are a standardized status view, exception report, management summary, and provenance. Exclusions include authoritative writes, workflow replacement, enterprise rollout, consequential decisions, and unlimited integrations. Implementation is bounded to 45 modeled days.

Support is annual but bounded to schema/access issues, mapping changes, report defects, configuration drift, and user questions. Acceptance requires processing the approved export, correct reconciliation, expected exceptions, a delivered summary, and no source mutation. These explicit boundaries reuse Chapters 5–7 rather than inventing another technical product.

## 4. What value remains in scope

The baseline fixture assigns **42%** of the original **$104,002.80** recoverable annual value to this slice: **$43,681.18**. This `MODELED ASSUMPTION` is neither realized savings nor willingness to pay. It explicitly leaves 58% of the original opportunity outside the engagement.

## 5. The smaller buying journey

The Chapter 2 journey type represents ten explicit stages. They total 62 active hours including four implementation-stage coordination hours, **58 acquisition hours**, and **115 sequential modeled days** including the 45-day implementation. Purchasing authorization and contract work remain visible. This is smaller than the 192-hour, 270-day formal RFP, not frictionless.

## 6. Stakeholder changes

Chapter 3 identities are reused: Operations Manager, permitting staff, Department Director, IT, security, procurement, and legal/contracts. Participation is narrower than the broad motion, but sponsorship does not replace access, purchasing, security, or contract authority. No real locality structure is asserted.

## 7. Delivery economics

Seven named work items—ingestion, mapping, reconciliation, exception reporting, summary, provenance/testing, and handoff—sum to **120 engineering hours**. At the inherited **$110/hour** engineering cost assumption, delivery labor is **$13,200**. The hours derive from scope items rather than a percentage of Chapter 0.

## 8. Acquisition economics

Applying the inherited sales and solutions rates to each journey stage produces **$5,970** acquisition labor cost. At the $30,000 implementation price, the ratio is 19.90%; acquisition effort is 19.33 hours per $10,000 revenue. These are descriptive ratios, not a score.

## 9. Support economics

Baseline annual support is **$4,000 revenue**, **30 hours**, and **$3,300 modeled cost**, leaving $700 support contribution. Small does not mean support-free: schemas, access, mappings, configuration, reports, and questions can recur.

## 10. Customer economics

The $30,000 implementation plus $4,000 support gives **$34,000 first-year cost**, **$9,681.18 net recoverable value**, and **8.24-month implementation-only payback**. Customer economics pass independently of seller target economics.

## 11. Seller break-even price

The lab's `MODELED ASSUMPTION` requires $10,000 implementation contribution. Therefore:

```text
seller break-even price
= delivery labor + acquisition labor + direct cost + required contribution
= $13,200 + $5,970 + $1,000 + $10,000
= $30,170
```

The result is an `OBSERVED LAB RESULT` given those inputs, not a market price.

## 12. Customer-supported price

The fictional customer rule requires nonnegative first-year net recoverable value. Consequently the maximum modeled implementation price is annual value addressed minus support: **$43,681.18 − $4,000 = $39,681.18**. It is not measured willingness to pay.

## 13. When the two prices cross

Break-even and customer-supported prices are independently derived. In the baseline, a theoretical price band exists, yet the actual $30,000 price is $170 below seller sustainability. The project passes while target viability fails: `POOR TARGET CUSTOMER`. The model does not repair this by assuming a price increase.

## 14. Sensitivity: too small

Three users, an exception list, 20% of original value, 48 engineering hours, $12,000 price, and $2,500 support reduce delivery. The same required stages preserve **58 acquisition hours**. Contribution becomes **−$250**; seller break-even is **$22,250**, above the **$18,300.56** customer-supported price. Delivery economics fail, so the result is `NO DEAL` rather than merely a poor target.

## 15. Sensitivity: efficient small engagement

The same scope, price, value, engineering, and support remain. Coordinated scheduling, pre-agreed scope, and narrower read-only review reduce named stage effort to **36 acquisition hours**; no vehicle or partner is assumed. Contribution rises to **$12,020**, seller break-even falls to **$27,980**, both gates pass, and the restrained verdict is `PROMISING — VALIDATE IN DISCOVERY`.

## 16. Sensitivity: high support burden

The baseline delivery scope remains immutable while recurring support doubles to **60 hours**. Its $6,600 cost exceeds $4,000 revenue. Support viability fails, producing `NO DEAL`, even though implementation contribution is unchanged.

## 17. Is there a viable middle band?

The fixture produces a middle band: the baseline narrowly misses seller sustainability, an even smaller engagement fails structurally, and a mechanism-specific acquisition reduction works. Contract size alone determines none of those outcomes.

## 18. What Chapter 8 demonstrates

Compared with the broad formal RFP (522 engineering hours, 192 acquisition hours, negative $60 contribution) and cooperative pilot (140 engineering hours, 58 pre-authorization hours, $13,310 contribution), this small baseline substantially lowers delivery but does not clear the contribution rule. Acquisition effort is calculated from the same reusable stage structure (`OBSERVED IMPLEMENTATION STRUCTURE`). Smaller scope therefore **complicates** the cookbook hypothesis: it can help, hurt, or work in a middle band depending on explicit acquisition and support mechanisms.

## 19. What Chapter 8 does not prove

It proves no universal minimum government contract size, willingness to pay, stage effort, labor cost, payback threshold, or procurement practice. It does not model a partner, prime contractor, existing vehicle, full governance workload, opportunity cost, throughput, cross-customer repeatability, or a capstone.

## 20. Why the larger-contract experiment comes next

Chapter 8 leaves the opposite question unresolved: whether more revenue can absorb acquisition and delivery costs without exceeding customer-supported economics or expanding risk and authority. That is a future experiment. **Chapter 9 is not implemented here.**
