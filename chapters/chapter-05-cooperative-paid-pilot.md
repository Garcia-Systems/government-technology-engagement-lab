# Chapter 5 — Cooperative Paid Pilot

## 1. The first attempt to break the verdict

Chapter 4 found a viable fictional project but a `POOR TARGET CUSTOMER` under `FORMAL_RFP`. Chapter 5 holds the James River County Permitting Department and core administrative problem recognizable while changing one central variable: the commercial engagement motion. All customer, process, threshold, labor, and value inputs remain fictional `MODELED ASSUMPTION`s.

```text
SAME DEPARTMENT + SAME CORE PROBLEM + BOUNDED COOPERATIVE PAID PILOT = ?
```

## 2. What makes a pilot a real engagement

This is a **$36,000 paid**, 90-day, bounded, measurable engagement—not free consulting and not the full project disguised as a pilot. It is reversible where practical, has explicit criteria, includes $4,000 of pilot-period support within the price, and ends with a results handoff. Neither acceptance nor payment implies expansion.

## 3. What the pilot includes

The executable fixture limits work to one permitting operations team, eight modeled users, one status-reconciliation slice, a synthetic or separately approved bounded export, an internal operational summary, and a 90-day execution and handoff. The intervention performs normalization and reconciliation only far enough to measure the commercial experiment.

## 4. What the pilot excludes

Inspectable exclusions are authoritative-system replacement, consequential automated writes, enterprise rollout, unlimited integrations, indefinite support, and open-ended custom requests. These exclusions prevent scope from silently becoming the 522-hour implementation.

## 5. The cooperative sponsor

The Chapter 3 `OPERATIONS_MANAGER` is a strong sponsor who coordinates scope and users. The department director still authorizes purchase; IT still gates technical access; security, procurement, legal/contracts, and accessibility retain their own participation. Strong coordination narrows friction; it does not confer magical authority.

## 6. The pilot buying journey

The reusable Chapter 2 journey structure represents sponsor identification, bounded discovery, scope agreement, technical validation, security/access review, purchasing path, pilot proposal, agreement/authorization, implementation, measurement, and acceptance. Pre-authorization consumes **58 active seller hours** and **75 modeled elapsed days**. The complete sequential lab journey is 110 modeled days. These are fictional allocations, not procurement rules.

## 7. The bounded technical implementation

Ten synthetic status events cover ordinary progression, one repeated status, a correction/resubmission, an unknown status, and a missing identifier. The implementation rejects exceptions, suppresses a repeated current status, and produces one latest-status operational view per valid permit. It is deliberately not a permitting product or Chapter 6 architecture experiment.

## 8. Acceptance criteria

Seven `MODELED ASSUMPTION` thresholds require: 10 records processed; at least one duplicate action avoided; exactly two exceptions surfaced; at least three status views; at least three manual lookups reduced; at least three report-preparation actions reduced; and at least six modeled users completing. All passing yields `PILOT_ACCEPTED`; one miss yields `PILOT_CONDITIONAL`; more yields `PILOT_FAILED`. Acceptance vocabulary is separate from commercial verdict vocabulary.

## 9. Synthetic execution results

Deterministic `OBSERVED LAB RESULT`s are 10 records processed, seven reconciliations, one duplicate action avoided, two exceptions surfaced, three latest-status views, three modeled manual lookups reduced, three modeled report actions reduced, and six modeled users completing. The result is `PILOT_ACCEPTED`. These fixture results say nothing about actual department performance.

## 10. Translating lab behavior into modeled economics

The observed count of seven avoided actions (one duplicate plus three lookup plus three reporting actions), combined with the `MODELED ASSUMPTION`s of four minutes per action and $36/hour, produces a **$16.80 modeled economic result** for those fixture actions. The separately modeled $39,000 expected benefit is an engagement assumption, not an extrapolation masquerading as observation.

## 11. Customer economics

Pilot price is **$36,000**, including **$4,000** pilot-period support. The pilot potentially affects **$52,000 annualized value**, less than the **$104,002.80 full-opportunity recoverable annual value**, but does not claim to capture it. A modeled **$39,000 measurable pilot benefit** produces **$3,000 customer pilot net benefit**. This is pilot value, not full-project value.

## 12. Seller economics

Chapter 4 rates remain sales $85/hour, solutions $125/hour, and engineering $110/hour. The pilot independently models 140 delivery hours, 58 acquisition hours, and $1,000 other direct cost. Deterministic costs are **$15,400 delivery** and **$6,290 acquisition**, yielding **$13,310 acquisition-adjusted contribution** and a **36.97% margin**. This simplified contribution is not company profit.

Context matters: acquisition is 16.11 hours per $10,000 revenue, delivery is 38.89 hours per $10,000, acquisition cost is 17.47% of revenue, and annualized opportunity value addressed is 50.00% of the full modeled recoverable value. These ratios are descriptive; there is no weighted score.

## 14. Sensitivity: too small

Reducing price to $12,000 and delivery to 70 hours makes the engagement easier to contemplate but leaves contribution below the fictional $10,000 sustainability minimum. Customer economics can pass while target economics fail: `POOR TARGET CUSTOMER`.

## 15. Sensitivity: too broad

Expanding engineering to 400 hours and multiplying governance/acquisition effort increases authorization delay and makes the motion resemble the full project. Even with $65,000 price and broader value addressed, contribution falls below the minimum: `POOR TARGET CUSTOMER`.

## 16. Sensitivity: weak sponsor

Keeping technical scope while limiting sponsor strength adds coordination work and delay across every pre-authorization stage. The target gate fails. A bounded artifact alone is insufficient; cooperation is a commercial condition.

## 17. Does the verdict change?

Yes, within this fictional lab. Project and target gates pass, acceptance is `PILOT_ACCEPTED`, and the derived commercial verdict is `PILOT-FIRST TARGET`: direct broad engagement remains unattractive or unproven, but a bounded paid entry motion is worth validating.

```text
SAME PROBLEM + DIFFERENT ENGAGEMENT MOTION = DIFFERENT TARGET RESULT
```

That sentence is an `OBSERVED LAB RESULT` given modeled inputs, not empirical proof about government markets. The cookbook's poor-target hypothesis is **WEAKENED UNDER THIS FICTIONAL PILOT MOTION**, not disproved generally.

## 18. Pilot success is not full-project approval

`PILOT_ACCEPTED` sets `full_implementation_authorized = false` and produces only `VALIDATE EXPANSION`. Expansion may require a new procurement, contract, larger security/accessibility review, production access, broader alignment, and ongoing support decision. Implementation acceptance and target classification never share an enum.

## 19. What Chapter 5 demonstrates

A paid boundary can reduce capital at risk, acquisition hours, delay, delivery effort, dependencies, and support exposure while preserving measurable modeled value. It also demonstrates that too-small, too-broad, and weak-sponsor variants can lose that advantage. Evidence, not the pilot label, decides.

## 20. What remains unresolved

Synthetic acceptance does not establish production reliability, repeatability, data access, real adoption, expansion economics, larger contracting feasibility, or ongoing support sustainability. No real procurement rule is inferred.

## 21. Why read-only architecture comes next

Chapter 5 deliberately tests the commercial motion, not a read-only-before-write architecture. A later chapter may isolate whether architecture further changes governance and risk. That experiment remains entirely unimplemented here.
