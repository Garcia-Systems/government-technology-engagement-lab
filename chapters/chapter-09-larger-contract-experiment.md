# Chapter 9 — Larger Contract Experiment

## 1. The opposite of the small-contract experiment

Chapter 8 asked whether shrinking a bounded engagement made it easier to approve. Chapter 9 holds the wholly fictional customer and opportunity value constant and asks the opposite: can **broader, justified value** carry semi-fixed acquisition cost? This is a lab mechanism, not government pricing guidance.

```text
SMALL CONTRACT                    LARGER JUSTIFIED CONTRACT
    |                                  |
    v                                  v
LOW REVENUE                       MORE SUPPORTED VALUE
    |                                  |
    +--- ACQUISITION FLOOR             v
    |                             MORE REVENUE
    v                                  +--- ACQUISITION FLOOR
TIGHT / NEGATIVE ECONOMICS             +--- INCREMENTAL DELIVERY
                                       v
                                  DO ECONOMICS IMPROVE?
```

## 2. Why bigger can help

Sponsor coordination, authorization, discovery, procurement, and contracting contain semi-fixed work. More supported implementation revenue can reduce acquisition cost/revenue and acquisition hours per $10,000. These are descriptive ratios, never a score.

## 3. Why bigger can hurt

More workflows can require more engineering, validation, stakeholders, access, acceptance, support, and change control. Therefore **bigger deal does not imply better deal**, and **more revenue does not imply more contribution**.

## 4. Building a justified larger scope

The main case retains Chapter 8's correction/resubmission reporting edge, then adds three decomposable components: a second workflow, management reporting/reconciliation, and a narrow read-only exception residual. It grows from 8 to 22 users and adds one approved intake export. It remains a bounded departmental solution—not enterprise transformation.

## 5. Incremental value accounting

The value ladder begins with Chapter 8's $43,681.176 modeled value. The second workflow adds $17,000, management reporting adds $12,000, and the bounded residual adds $8,000. Each component names burden categories, overlap, and remaining residual. Sequential accumulation reaches **$80,681.176**, leaving **$23,321.624** of the unchanged **$104,002.80** opportunity. All allocations are `MODELED ASSUMPTION`; the totals are `OBSERVED LAB RESULT`.

## 6. Acquisition floor plus incremental acquisition work

The explicit 58-hour floor comprises sponsor/authorization, discovery/validation, and procurement/contract coordination. Added workflow discovery (8h), reporting requirements (5h), and technical validation (6h) bring the main case to **77 hours**. Acquisition neither stays fixed nor scales linearly with value.

## 7. Delivery scaling

Chapter 8's 120-hour delivery is retained. Named configuration, workflow testing, reports, reconciliation testing, exception handling, documentation, deployment, and training add 125 hours, for **245 engineering hours**. There is no project-size multiplier.

## 8. Governance scaling

The added components expose workflow ownership, acceptance matrices, report approval, an approved export, and change control. Price does not erase those surfaces.

## 9. Support scaling

The model exposes 55 annual support hours for workflow mappings, reporting configurations, mapping changes, monitoring, and exceptions. At the inherited $110 engineering cost rate, cost is **$6,050**, alongside **$8,000** modeled support revenue.

## 10. Seller price floor

The `MODELED SUSTAINABILITY FLOOR` is delivery labor + acquisition labor + other direct cost + the inherited $10,000 minimum contribution. For the main case: $26,950 + $8,105 + $2,500 + $10,000 = **$47,555**. This is not “profit” or a real benchmark.

## 11. Customer price ceiling

The fictional customer rule requires nonnegative first-year recoverable value. With support separate, ceiling = value addressed − annual support = $80,681.176 − $8,000 = **$72,681.176**. It is independent of seller costs and is not willingness-to-pay evidence.

## 12. The viable price corridor

Corridor = customer ceiling − seller floor. The main case produces **$25,126.176**, classified `VIABLE_CORRIDOR`. The $5,000 fragile boundary is a `MODELED ASSUMPTION`, not a score.

```text
SELLER PRICE FLOOR $47,555
        |
        |<---- $25,126.176 VIABLE CORRIDOR ---->|
                                                    |
                              CUSTOMER PRICE CEILING $72,681.176
```

A negative corridor is printed as negative dollars and `NO_CORRIDOR`; no corridor diagram is implied for that ordering.

## 13. Small versus larger engagement

Small is $43,681.176 value / $30,000 price / 120 engineering hours / 58 acquisition hours. Its acquisition cost is $5,970 (19.90% of revenue), seller floor $30,170, ceiling $39,681.176, and contribution $9,830: `POOR TARGET CUSTOMER`. The justified larger case is $80,681.176 / $65,000 / 245h / 77h. Acquisition cost is $8,105 (12.47%), contribution is $27,445, and both gates pass: `PROMISING — VALIDATE IN DISCOVERY`. Scale improves this fictional acquisition-adjusted result while adding real delivery and governance burden.

## 14. Sensitivity: price without value

The price-only case retains $43,681.176 value but charges $50,000 plus $8,000 support. Seller economics improve, but customer net value becomes negative. Project failure takes precedence: `NO DEAL`. Price alone cannot repair the opportunity.

## 15. Sensitivity: transformation overreach

A weakly justified expansion adds only $14,000 value while reaching 530 engineering hours, 118 acquisition hours, 120 support hours, expanded governance, and an $85,000 price. Its floor is $86,530, ceiling $45,681.176, corridor **−$40,848.824**, and verdict `NO DEAL`. Scope grows faster than value.

## 16. Sensitivity: acquisition-efficient larger contract

Clear requirements and sponsor coordination reduce the explicit floor from 58 to 38 hours and apply a 60% factor to the named incremental work, producing 49 acquisition hours and an 88-day cycle. This `SENSITIVITY ASSUMPTION` assumes no contract vehicle and introduces no partner motion.

## 17. Does scale absorb procurement friction?

In the main fictional case, yes, partially: acquisition hours rise but acquisition ratios fall, and supported value leaves pricing room. The result depends on component economics rather than contract size as a category.

## 18. What Chapter 9 demonstrates

A coherent value ladder can improve acquisition-adjusted economics while customer economics remain viable. Incremental value must exceed incremental economic and governance burden. The Chapter 8 `POOR TARGET CUSTOMER` hypothesis is **further complicated and weakened for this supported larger scope**, not universally disproved.

## 19. What Chapter 9 does not prove

It does not establish market willingness to pay, an optimal size, a procurement method, real labor rates, a government benchmark, repeatability, throughput, full security/accessibility workload, or enterprise suitability.

## 20. Why partner/channel motion comes next

A future chapter may ask whether another commercial route changes acquisition mechanics. Chapter 9 does not implement it: no prime contractor, partner, channel, incumbent vehicle, or Chapter 10 verdict exists here.
