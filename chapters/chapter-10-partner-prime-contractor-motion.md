# Chapter 10 — Partner / Prime-Contractor Motion

> **Fiction notice.** Harbor Civic Solutions, James River County Permitting Department, every person, price, fee, capability, relationship, and result in this chapter are fictional educational model inputs. Nothing describes a real contractor, government, procurement norm, or market benchmark.

## 1. What if customer access belongs to someone else?

Chapters 0–9 varied gates, journey, topology, procurement motion, technical authority, configuration, and scope. Chapter 10 holds the underlying **$104,002.80 recoverable annual value**, **$102,000 first-year customer contract**, and **522 engineering hours** comparable to the Formal RFP while changing who owns customer access and contracting.

```text
DIRECT                              PARTNER-LED

SELLER                              SELLER
  |                                   |
  +--> CUSTOMER ACCESS                v
  +--> PROCUREMENT                  PARTNER / PRIME
  +--> CONTRACT                       |
  +--> TECHNICAL DELIVERY             +--> CUSTOMER ACCESS
  |                                   +--> PROCUREMENT
  v                                   +--> CONTRACT
CUSTOMER                              |
                                      v
                                    CUSTOMER

                            SELLER STILL OWNS TECHNICAL DELIVERY
```

## 2. Direct acquisition versus partner-led acquisition

The direct Formal RFP charges all 192 acquisition hours to the seller. The partner motion retains every legitimate stage but assigns a primary owner and explicit retained seller input. It deterministically leaves **91 seller acquisition hours**. A channel changes commercial mechanics; it does not make security review, accessibility response, technical validation, customer IT, access approval, stakeholder alignment, implementation, or acceptance disappear.

## 3. Meet the fictional partner

**Harbor Civic Solutions** is a `FICTIONAL_PRIME_CONTRACTOR`, introduced only as a `MODELED ALTERNATIVE ASSUMPTION`. The fixture assumes an existing relationship, opportunity access, procurement familiarity, prime-contract administration, billing, project management, and coordination. These are not observations about a real organization.

## 4. What the partner owns

The partner owns the initial customer relationship, account qualification, opportunity access, procurement coordination, prime contract, invoicing, commercial project management, stakeholder coordination, and first-line support. Its first-year compensation is **18% of the fictional $102,000 contract**, or **$18,360**. The percentage is not a claimed customary margin.

## 5. What the technical seller still owns

The neutral `TECHNICAL_DELIVERY_SUBCONTRACTOR` role owns technical discovery, design, configuration/custom delivery, testing, documentation, implementation and escalation support, security/accessibility input, and estimates. Delivery remains **522 engineering hours / $57,420 modeled internal cost**.

## 6. Stage ownership

The executable stage vocabulary is `PARTNER`, `SELLER`, `CUSTOMER`, and `JOINT`. Partner-led opportunity discovery and procurement coexist with joint requirements, security, accessibility, pricing, and clarifications; seller-led solution design, response, and implementation planning; and customer-owned evaluation, selection, and authorization. Ownership reduces seller effort without deleting the stage.

```text
PARTNER IDENTIFIES + QUALIFIES
        ↓
JOINT TECHNICAL DISCOVERY
        ↓
SELLER TECHNICAL VALIDATION / RESPONSE
        ↓
PARTNER PROCUREMENT + SELLER INPUT
        ↓
PRIME CONTRACT + SUBCONTRACT WORK ORDER
        ↓
SELLER IMPLEMENTATION
        ↓
CUSTOMER ACCEPTANCE
```

Chapter 3 government identities remain government stakeholders. `HARBOR_CIVIC_SOLUTIONS` and `TECHNICAL_SELLER` are external commercial participants, not government authorities.

## 7. Customer and contract ownership

The baseline relationship is `PARTNER_OWNED`; contract ownership is `PARTNER`. Consequently renewal and expansion depend on the partner, direct account knowledge and cross-sell access are limited, and reference ownership remains unresolved. No arbitrary dollar value is assigned to those effects.

## 8. Channel economics

The customer still pays **$102,000** and receives the same modeled **$104,002.80** value, leaving **$2,002.80** first-year net recoverable value. Revenue splitting does not improve customer economics.

The seller receives **$83,640 modeled seller engagement revenue** after the **$18,360 partner share**. Against that are **$57,420 delivery**, **$11,055 retained acquisition**, **$660 retained project management**, and **$3,300 escalation-support** costs. The deterministic contribution is **$11,205 (13.40%)**, above Chapter 4's fictional $10,000 sustainability floor.

Partner economics expose only the fee and responsibilities assumed; they do not claim to model partner profitability.

## 9. Acquisition savings

Seller effort falls from **192 to 91 hours**, saving **101 hours**. Category rates inherited from Chapter 4 reduce acquisition cost from **$20,640 to $11,055**, a **$9,585** saving. Technical response, architecture, security/accessibility input, scoping, estimates, and meetings remain charged.

## 10. Channel cost

The narrow labor comparison is:

```text
$9,585 acquisition cost saved
- $18,360 channel cost
= -$8,775 net channel economic effect
```

That negative measure matters, but it is not the verdict by itself: the modeled partner also enables access that is `LIMITED` directly. Access enablement and loss of control remain qualitative reason codes, never a weighted score.

```text
ACQUISITION SAVINGS
        -
CHANNEL COST
        +
ACCESS ENABLEMENT
        -
LOSS OF CONTROL
        =
PARTNER MOTION TRADEOFF
```

## 11. Support responsibility

The modeled path is `CUSTOMER → PARTNER FIRST-LINE → TECHNICAL SELLER ESCALATION`. The seller receives **$19,680** of the $24,000 support portion embedded in seller engagement revenue and bears **30 hours / $3,300** of escalation effort. First-line ownership does not eliminate technical support.

The contribution views are labeled rather than silently treated as identical: Chapter 4 reports implementation contribution while Chapter 10 reports first-year engagement contribution because the partner share applies to the complete first-year contract and the seller retains support obligations.

## 13. Sensitivity: channel fee too high

Raising the fictional share to **35%** while holding access and savings constant makes seller delivery economics fail. Gate precedence produces `NO DEAL`; access cannot repair a foundational project failure.

## 14. Sensitivity: high-value access

Changing direct access from `LIMITED` to `NO` changes no technical value, customer price, engineering scope, or partner fee. The scenario remains `PARTNER-LED TARGET` and makes the distinction between theoretical direct economics and an actually accessible opportunity explicit.

## 15. Sensitivity: partner adds little

The fee remains 18%, but seller hours rise on partner-owned stages. Acquisition savings shrink while channel cost stays fixed. This tests an extra-layer partner rather than assuming channel leverage; its verdict follows the executable economics.

## 16. Can weaker margin still be a better motion?

Yes only in the bounded logical sense demonstrated here: direct access is weak, customer economics pass, seller economics clear the modeled floor, and partner access is credible by assumption. This is not a claim that lower margin is generally preferable or that fictional access has been validated in a market.

## 17. The `PARTNER-LED TARGET` verdict

The verdict means: **this fictional category remains weak through direct Formal RFP acquisition but is worth validating only through the modeled partner-led motion**. It requires project gates to pass, the direct target to fail, partner economics to pass, and direct access to be limited or absent. A customer- or delivery-economics failure always returns `NO DEAL`.

## 18. What Chapter 10 demonstrates

The code can represent stage ownership, commercial/customer relationship ownership, retained seller effort, channel share, support escalation, descriptive dependencies, and gate precedence. For this fixture the motion **changes** the cookbook hypothesis from an unqualified direct `POOR TARGET CUSTOMER` to a conditional channel-only target.

## 19. What Chapter 10 does not prove

It proves no real access, channel percentage, procurement path, partner performance, renewal behavior, market size, repeatability, or government attractiveness. It has no weighted partner score and implements no incumbent alternative, throughput model, security/governance expansion, or Chapter 11 contract vehicle.

## 20. Why an existing purchasing path comes next

The unresolved question is whether an existing purchasing path changes authorization mechanics beyond relationship ownership. That belongs to Chapter 11 and is deliberately not implemented here. Chapter 10 stops at the partner/prime motion.
