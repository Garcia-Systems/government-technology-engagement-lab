# Chapter 14 — Incumbent Vendor Alternative

> **Fiction notice:** CivicFlow Permitting Suite, CivicFlow Operations Module, the provider, every capability, limitation, price, service, access statement, support term, and roadmap statement in this chapter are wholly fictional **MODELED ALTERNATIVE ASSUMPTION**s. Nothing describes a real permitting vendor.

## 1. Custom software has to beat the alternative

```text
CUSTOM SOFTWARE OPPORTUNITY
        ↓
INCUMBENT SUPPORTED ALTERNATIVE EXISTS?
        ↓
COMPARE VALUE + COST + RISK + COVERAGE
        ↓
DOES CUSTOM STILL DESERVE TO EXIST?
```

The lab compares `DO NOTHING`, `CONFIGURE`, `INCUMBENT MODULE`, `INCUMBENT SERVICES`, `NARROW CUSTOM EDGE`, and `BROAD CUSTOM`. It uses no weighted score and gives neither custom nor incumbent capability automatic precedence.

## 2. Meet the fictional incumbent module

The serious modeled alternative is the **CivicFlow Operations Module (fictional)**. It supplies improved status workflow, correction/resubmission tracking, configurable dashboards, scheduled reports, queues, notifications, duplicate warnings, a supported export/API surface, and incumbent support. These are assumptions, not product research.

## 3. What the incumbent already owns

The module uses the fictional platform's native data model, authentication, access control, audit history, deployment path, and vendor support. This is a **technical alternative advantage**. The fixture separately assumes acquisition viability; that **commercial/acquisition advantage** is not inferred from native access.

## 4. Capability coverage

The six Chapter 7 categories reconcile to $104,002.80: duplicate entry, status reconciliation, report preparation, lookup/search, correction administration, and workflow rework. Each alternative names categories; a set-based calculation counts each category once. Value addressed is their sum, residual is total recoverable value minus that sum, and percentage addressed uses the unchanged total. Calculated quantities are **OBSERVED LAB RESULT**s.

## 5. Limitations

The module has limited cross-source reconciliation and historical normalization, constrained reporting customization, no external orchestration, a fixed user experience, a vendor-controlled roadmap, and limited custom exception logic. This makes it credible rather than perfect.

## 6. Incumbent professional services

`INCUMBENT SERVICES` is distinct from the module alone. Its $58,000 implementation line models workflow setup, mapping, reports, training, and supported integration setup; its separate $18,000 recurring module/license/support line remains visible:

```text
MODULE / LICENSE + PROFESSIONAL SERVICES
```

The lab models only customer cost, not CivicFlow profitability.

## 7. Customer economics

For every option the executable reports annual value addressed, residual, implementation and recurring costs, their first-year total, first-year net recoverable value, implementation-only payback, full first-year-cost payback, and coverage percentage. Different commercial line items are normalized through customer totals; a custom implementation and a module license are not pretended to be identical.

## 8. Support ownership

Native alternatives assign support to the incumbent; custom alternatives retain a custom seller obligation. `DO NOTHING` leaves support with the customer. Provider effort is descriptive; seller economics are calculated only for custom references.

## 9. Technical-access advantage

Chapter 13's rule remains binding. Unsupported third-party access is never bypassed. The incumbent can have supported access to its own native system while third-party write/API access is unavailable, but that does not imply that every field or external source is exposed.

## 10. Governance advantage

Chapter 12's configuration-first surface shifts authentication, credential handling, audit logging, environment restriction, and retention implementation to the incumbent; it does not erase approval, configuration review, accessibility acceptance, change authorization, or support planning. Read-only and write-capable custom surfaces retain their respective Chapter 12 ownership.

## 11. Configuration versus module

Configuration reuses Chapter 7's semantics and its $44,000 implementation/$6,000 annual-support structure. The module is separately modeled at $32,000 implementation plus $18,000 recurring. Both can be adequate; the comparison exposes what each solves rather than declaring a product hierarchy.

## 12. Incumbent versus broad custom

Broad custom preserves Chapter 0/4's $78,000 implementation, $24,000 support, 522 engineering hours, 192 acquisition hours, full burden coverage, access dependency, and failed acquisition target gate. Flexibility does not confer superiority. Conversely, native safety does not repair negative customer economics.

## 13. Incumbent plus narrow custom edge

A read-only Chapter 6 edge remains available for duplicate-entry/status-reconciliation and related lookup residuals, but it is not automatically bundled. The decision first tests incumbent-only adequacy. Only a bounded, material, feasible, economic, supportable residual earns `NARROW CUSTOM EDGE`.

## 14. Strong-incumbent sensitivity

The strong scenario covers all six categories, lowers modeled implementation price to $24,000, and shortens duration to 40 days. It derives `CONFIGURE / BUY`.

## 15. Weak-incumbent sensitivity

The weak-native sensitivity covers only status reconciliation, report preparation, and lookup/search. Its material residual makes the independently feasible read-only edge the derived `NARROW CUSTOM EDGE` result.

## 16. Expensive-incumbent sensitivity

Capability stays unchanged while module implementation rises to $95,000 and recurring cost to $35,000. Its own economics fail. The comparison can still find another adequate native path; technical advantage alone is not economic superiority.

## 17. Incumbent-only-supported-access sensitivity

Third-party supported access is unavailable under Chapter 13 semantics while strong native access remains. Custom alternatives are infeasible rather than permitted to bypass controls; the adequate native module derives `CONFIGURE / BUY`.

## 18. When CONFIGURE / BUY wins

The transparent fictional adequacy rule requires **at least 75% coverage and no more than $25,000 residual**. Selection precedence is: (1) feasible, (2) nonnegative customer economics, (3) adequate coverage, (4) supportable, (5) acquisition viable, then (6) prefer lower custom ownership for adequate outcomes. This is a **MODELED ASSUMPTION**, not a buyer standard.

## 19. When custom still deserves to exist

Custom survives only for a responsible residual or when a broader custom project independently passes access, customer, seller, support, and acquisition gates. If native capability is inadequate, access is closed, and remaining economics/support fail, `NO DEAL` remains available.

```text
CUSTOM SOFTWARE
DOES NOT WIN
BECAUSE IT IS CUSTOM

IT WINS ONLY IF
THE ALTERNATIVES
LEAVE A MATERIAL
RESPONSIBLE RESIDUAL
```

## 20. What Chapter 14 demonstrates

The credible baseline module addresses five categories, leaving duplicate entry as an $18,000 residual; 82.69% coverage, $50,000 first-year cost, and $36,002.80 net are deterministic lab results. The adequacy rule therefore selects `CONFIGURE / BUY`. Strong, weak, expensive, and closed-access changes show the result is derived.

## 21. What Chapter 14 does not prove

It proves nothing about real products, pricing, procurement, buyer preferences, compliance, or vendor quality. It does not estimate incumbent profitability, use a weighted score, claim native capability is universally safer, or claim customization is universally more valuable.

## 22. Why acquisition economics comes next

Chapter 14 leaves unresolved how alternative-specific purchasing routes, renewal structure, price escalation, authorization work, and opportunity-level acquisition cost should be synthesized. Those are Chapter 15 questions. Chapter 15 is intentionally not implemented here.
