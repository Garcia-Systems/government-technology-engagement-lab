# Chapter 15 — Acquisition Economics

## 1. The cost of winning the work

Chapter 15 asks a seller-side question: **what did it cost to win this successful engagement?** Acquisition cost is explicit labor attribution, not a sales score or a market benchmark:

```text
ACQUISITION COST = Σ(seller-owned acquisition hours × internal seller cost rate)
                   + other explicit acquisition costs
```

All amounts remain fictional modeled assumptions or deterministic lab results.

## 2. Acquisition is not delivery

```text
CUSTOMER VALUE
      |
      v
ENGAGEMENT PRICE
      |
      v
DELIVERY COST
      |
      v
DELIVERY CONTRIBUTION
      |
      v
ACQUISITION COST
      |
      v
ACQUISITION-ADJUSTED
CONTRIBUTION
```

Delivery labor makes the implementation. Acquisition labor wins and authorizes it. Support and other direct obligations remain separate line items. “Contribution” is a simplified modeled measure, **not profit**.

## 3. Acquisition is not elapsed time

```text
ACTIVE SALES WORK
        ≠
ELAPSED SALES CYCLE

HOURS
consume labor capacity

MONTHS
consume calendar/pipeline capacity
```

The Formal RFP has 192 active hours and 270 modeled days. Days are displayed but never multiplied by an hourly rate.

## 4. Acquisition work taxonomy

The reusable taxonomy is `PROSPECTING`, `QUALIFICATION`, `DISCOVERY`, `MEETINGS`, `TECHNICAL_VALIDATION`, `SECURITY_APPROVAL_SUPPORT`, `ACCESSIBILITY_APPROVAL_SUPPORT`, `PROPOSAL`, `PRICING`, `PROCUREMENT_SUPPORT`, `CONTRACT_COORDINATION`, `IMPLEMENTATION_PLANNING`, and `PARTNER_COORDINATION`. Each item records its ID, motion, seller/partner/customer ownership, seller hours and rate, computed seller cost, evidence, and source reference.

## 5. Formal RFP attribution

The 18 Chapter 4 stages are referenced once and reconcile to **192 hours / $20,640**. Against $78,000 implementation revenue and $57,420 delivery labor, delivery contribution is $20,580 and acquisition-adjusted contribution is **-$60**. The 270-day cycle has no automatic dollar value.

## 6. Cooperative pilot attribution

The bounded authorization stages reconcile to **58 hours / $6,290**. Narrow discovery, validation, proposal, and approval reduce work, but revenue is also only $36,000. Delivery contribution is $20,600; after acquisition and $1,000 other direct cost, acquisition-adjusted contribution is **$13,310**.

## 7. Partner-led attribution

The seller bears **91 hours / $11,055**; explicitly assigned partner work is 93 hours and customer-owned work is 8 hours. Seller economics use **$83,640 seller engagement revenue**, while the $102,000 customer contract remains visible. The 18% channel share reduces seller revenue; it is not added again as acquisition labor. Retained project management and seller escalation support remain direct costs. Acquisition-adjusted contribution is **$11,205**.

## 8. Existing-path attribution

The existing path removes or reduces competitive solicitation, proposal administration, evaluation, procurement, and contracting work while retaining discovery, technical validation, security, accessibility, pricing, and implementation planning. Acquisition falls to **114 hours / $13,050** without changing $78,000 price, 522 engineering hours, $57,420 delivery labor, or customer economics. Acquisition-adjusted contribution becomes **$7,530**.

## 9. Small versus larger contract

The small motion retains a 58-hour acquisition floor against $30,000 revenue: $5,970 acquisition cost and $9,830 acquisition-adjusted contribution. The justified larger contract has semi-fixed and incremental work—77 hours / $8,105—against $65,000 revenue and produces $27,445 acquisition-adjusted contribution. Larger is not automatically better; the scope must still support customer value, delivery, governance, and support.

## 10. Delivery contribution

`delivery contribution = seller engagement revenue − delivery labor cost`. It deliberately shows the economics before acquisition. Other direct costs remain visible below this subtotal.

## 11. Acquisition-adjusted contribution

`revenue − delivery labor − acquisition labor − other direct costs` standardizes the existing chapter calculations. Formal RFP moves from $20,580 before acquisition to -$60 after it; this is the central synthesis.

## 12. Acquisition efficiency ratios

The report exposes acquisition hours per $10,000 revenue, acquisition cost/revenue, acquisition cost/value addressed, acquisition hours/engineering hours, and acquisition cost/delivery cost. These are descriptive ratios—not a combined score, ranking, or benchmark.

## 13. Won-deal economics

Primary results are **won-deal acquisition economics**: earlier chapters modeled work consumed by successful engagements. The item architecture can later support opportunity analysis, but Chapter 15 does not assign win probabilities or expected pipeline value.

## 14. Lost-deal sensitivity

A limited `SENSITIVITY ASSUMPTION` sets Formal RFP implementation revenue to zero while retaining $20,640 already incurred acquisition cost. Opportunity contribution is therefore **-$20,640**. It is an exposure demonstration, not a funnel engine.

## 15. Where double counting can happen

Every item has one source reference. Chapter 12 governance approval work is attributed through the already-counted Chapter 4 journey stages; it is not appended. Partner labor is kept out of seller labor cost, while channel share is captured through seller revenue. Proposal, procurement, and contract work likewise appear once.

## 16. Good customer economics, bad seller economics

Formal RFP customer first-year net value remains $2,002.80 while seller acquisition-adjusted contribution is -$60. Existing-path attribution changes seller economics but leaves customer value and cost unchanged. Customer attractiveness and seller sustainability therefore remain independent.

## 17. What Chapter 15 demonstrates

Acquisition work is real cost. Formal proposal/technical/governance work drives the direct result; a pilot narrows it, a partner shifts ownership but charges through the channel, and an existing path reduces commercial administration without changing delivery. This makes `HIGH_SOLUTIONS_EFFORT` and `PROCUREMENT_DIFFICULTY` traceable rather than rhetorical.

## 18. What Chapter 15 does not prove

It does not prove typical government sales costs, causal win rates, market salaries, universal sustainability, or a customer-category verdict. Buyer access, technical feasibility, elapsed cycle, governance, support, and customer value remain independent constraints. There is no acquisition score.

## 19. Why throughput and opportunity cost come next

One won deal does not reveal how many opportunities a team can carry or what displaced work costs. Those are deliberately unresolved questions for Chapter 16. No throughput, capacity allocation, probability weighting, or portfolio model is implemented here.
